For integers n ≥ 0 and k ≥ 1 let v(n,k) be the number of prime factors of n+k that divide none of n, n+1, …, n+k−1, equivalently the number of primes p > k dividing n+k, and let v1(n) = supk≥1 v(n,k). Erdős and Selfridge (1967) expected v1(n) = 1 to have only finitely many solutions but could not prove it (Erdős Problem #889). We prove that every n ≥ N0, where log N0 = e45.28 ≈ 4.62×1019, has some k ≤ 10 log n with v(n,k) ≥ 2; hence every solution of v1(n) = 1 is smaller than N0. The proof combines an elementary counting lemma with Matveev’s explicit lower bound for linear forms in logarithms, and every numerical inequality is certified by interval arithmetic. The same argument gives, for all integers l ≥ 1 and n with log log n ≥ 48.82 and log n ≥ l, some k in [l, 10 l log n] with v(n,k) ≥ 2. The qualitative finiteness also follows from a 1981 theorem of Langevin combined with an elementary lemma.

What is new and what is not. The explicit constant N0 for Theorem A (log log N0 = 45.28) and the explicit uniform-in-l statement with C0 = 10 and N(l) = max(exp(e48.82), ⌈el⌉), together with sharper per-l thresholds for 1 ≤ l ≤ 20, are new as far as we could determine. The qualitative statement that {n : v1(n) = 1} is finite — with an effective but uncomputed bound — is not new: it follows from a 1981 theorem of Langevin on k-smooth integers among n+1, …, n+k combined with a short elementary lemma, a route this note writes out but did not discover from scratch. This note does not address the main problem v0(n) → ∞, nor vl(n) → ∞ for fixed l, nor any lower bound of 3 or more; N0 is far beyond computation, so the conjecture that 330 is the largest n with v1(n) = 1 remains open.
